# Methodology literature for a preregistered negative-result computational study

Context: study uses SESOI = 0.03 accuracy points, three-outcome CI verdicts, 4-8 test families per stage, staged design, independent reviews by sub-agents of the same model. Reading depth is stated per source ("full" = text was read through a fetch tool; "abstract/secondary" = only abstract or search snippets). Items I could not verify are listed under Gaps in each section.

## 1. Equivalence testing, SESOI, and "no evidence" vs "evidence of absence"

### Takeaway
Non-significance cannot support "no effect"; the accepted remedy is to prespecify a SESOI and show the CI (90% for alpha=.05 TOST) lies inside +/-SESOI. Our three-outcome CI verdict maps onto Lakens' four-outcome scheme, but the SESOI of 0.03 needs an explicit justification and a check of power/precision against it, and the wording "no evidence of an advantage" must be separated from "equivalent".

### Cited Findings
- Lakens 2017, "Equivalence tests: a practical primer", Soc Psychol Personal Sci 8(4):355-362, doi:10.1177/1948550617697177 (PMC5502906; full text read). States that absence of an effect "can be rejected, but not statistically supported" by a nonsignificant NHST result; that equivalence bounds should be a prespecified SESOI; that TOST requires both one-sided tests to be significant; that the 90% CI must exclude the bounds (95% CI excludes zero for NHST); and that results fall in four categories: equivalent and not different from zero, different from zero and not equivalent, different and equivalent, undetermined. Also: state the bounds whenever claiming "statistically equivalent", and report effect size estimates. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5502906/)
- Lakens, Scheel, Isager 2018, "Equivalence testing for psychological research: a tutorial", AMPPS 1(2):259-269, doi:10.1177/2515245918770963 (abstract/secondary only; publisher page returned 403). Abstract: psychologists must be able to test for absence as well as presence; TOST can reject the presence of a SESOI; covers several ways to justify a SESOI and worked examples of performing and reporting. — [CORE/Scinapse record via search](https://core.ac.uk/works/149799306); [publisher](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2515245918770963)
- Lakens's online textbook "Improving Your Statistical Inferences", equivalence chapter (full chapter read, first ~100k chars): justify SESOI from theory, anchor-based methods, cost-benefit, small telescopes, or minimal detectable effect; "clearly state" the SESOI; TOST is an intersection-union test so no correction for the two one-sided tests; if alpha is adjusted, use CI = 100 - 2*alpha; report bounds in raw units; avoid claiming "absence of an effect", call inconclusive results inconclusive; power depends on SESOI and assumed true effect, and assuming exactly zero true effect when a small one is plausible raises the chance of an inconclusive result. — [Lakens book ch. 9](https://lakens.github.io/statistical_inferences/09-equivalencetest.html)
- Lakens et al. 2018, "Justify your alpha", Nat Hum Behav 2:168-171, doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0311-x (abstract/secondary only). Core recommendation: transparently report and justify all design choices including alpha, rather than adopting a blanket threshold (e.g. 0.005). I could not confirm any specific multiple-comparison text. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/128599/) (accepted-version PDF listed there; not read)
- Caution on citation: arXiv 1709.07588, which is sometimes confused with it, is actually McShane, Gal, Gelman, Robert, Tackett, "Abandon Statistical Significance" (Am Stat 73:sup1, 235-245), a different paper that argues against fixed thresholds. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.07588)
- Agarwal et al. 2021 and Bouthillier et al. 2021 (see section 2) both argue for interval estimates and effect-size thresholds rather than binary significance, which is consistent with CI-based verdicts.

### Inferences
- A 0.03 SESOI is only defensible if justified (e.g. by test-set sampling noise, seed variance, or a cost-benefit or practical-relevance argument). Bouthillier et al. show benchmark sampling noise from test-set size is often comparable to published gains, so compute the binomial SE of accuracy at the actual test-set size and show that 0.03 is above the minimal detectable difference; otherwise "equivalent" verdicts will often be "inconclusive".
- Three-outcome verdict = Lakens outcomes collapsed: (a) CI inside +/-0.03 => equivalent (no meaningful advantage); (b) CI entirely above +0.03 (or exclusion of zero and beyond margin) => advantage; (c) CI straddles a bound => inconclusive. If the paper's headline is "no evidence of an advantage", state which of (a) or (c) occurred per comparison; (c) supports only "no evidence", not "evidence of absence". Use the 90% CI (or 100-2*alpha when alpha is corrected) for the equivalence call; mixing a 95% CI with equivalence bounds is a known error.
- Report bounds in raw accuracy points (0.03), not standardized units.
- A one-sided (non-inferiority/superiority) framing is more efficient if only "advantage" is of interest; Lakens notes directional equivalence is more efficient.

### Gaps
- Schuirmann 1987 (J Pharmacokinet Biopharm 15:657-680, TOST original) was not fetched; cite from Lakens' secondary account only.
- Full text of Lakens-Scheel-Isager 2018 and Justify Your Alpha not read.
- No source found giving a norm for what SESOI is "small enough" for ML accuracy; 0.03 must be justified internally.

## 2. Multiple comparisons, forking paths, and preregistration in computational/ML work

### Takeaway
The literature supports preregistration plus transparent deviation reporting, family-wise or FDR control within a defined family, and reporting uncertainty over many seeds/splits. None of the sources I read gives a rule for families spanning stages of a staged study; per-stage families leave cumulative error across stages uncontrolled, and testing the same hypothesis in several stages is a garden-of-forking-paths risk. That is a legitimate weakness to disclose.

### Cited Findings
- Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, Mellor 2018, "The preregistration revolution", PNAS 115(11):2600-2606, doi:10.1073/pnas.1708274114 (abstract/secondary only; PMC page was blocked by a browser check). Preregistration separates prediction from postdiction; hindsight bias makes confusing them easy; define questions and analysis plan before observing outcomes; strategies exist for cases with existing data. — [search summary](https://pubpeer.com/publications/E96C39735908C94D88D0481220182A); [PMC6233068](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233068)
- Gelman & Loken 2013/2014, "The garden of forking paths" (unpublished PDF; text not extractable; used search summaries): false positives can arise without conscious fishing because analysis choices depend on the data, even if the hypothesis is stated in advance, even with a single comparison per dataset. — [search summary / Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forking_paths_problem)
- Benjamini & Hochberg 1995, JRSS B 57:289-300, doi:10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x (abstract/secondary only): controls the expected proportion of false rejections (FDR) rather than FWER; proved for independent statistics. — [citedrive record](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/controlling-the-false-discovery-rate-a-practical-and-powerful-approach-to-multiple-testing)
- Holm 1979 (Scand J Stat 6:65-70): stepwise procedure controlling FWER, at least as powerful as Bonferroni (secondary, Wikipedia-level only). — [Wikipedia FDR page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_discovery_rate)
- Pineau et al., "Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research (NeurIPS 2019 Reproducibility Program)", JMLR 22(164), arXiv:2003.12206 (abstract only): code submission policy, a community reproducibility challenge, and a reproducibility checklist. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12206)
- Bouthillier et al. 2021, "Accounting for variance in ML benchmarks", MLSys 2021, arXiv:2103.03098 (ar5iv, ~100k of 160k chars read): data sampling is the dominant variance source; init variance is typically under 50% of that; HOpt variance is comparable to init; test-set size limits detection; recommend randomizing many sources, multiple data splits, and comparing by probability of outperforming P(A>B) with a bootstrap CI and threshold gamma=0.75. Simulation: single run ~10% false positives / 75% false negatives; average-difference test ~90% false negatives; P(A>B) ~5% / 30%. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03098)
- Agarwal et al. 2021, "Deep RL at the edge of the statistical precipice", NeurIPS, arXiv:2108.13264 (ar5iv, ~100k of 116k read): point estimates from 3-10 runs hide uncertainty; recommends stratified bootstrap CIs (percentile CIs worked with as few as 10 runs), IQM, performance profiles, probability of improvement, and avoiding dichotomous significance tests. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264)
- Henderson et al. 2018, "Deep RL that matters", AAAI, arXiv:1709.06560 (ar5iv read): two groups of five seeds with identical settings gave statistically different distributions (t=-9.09, p=0.0016); recommend many seeds, no cherry-picking, significance tests/CIs/bootstrap/power, tuned baselines, full reporting of hyperparameters. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)
- Lakens book (error-control chapter): the URL I tried returned 404, so no checked statement of family definition guidance. See Gaps.

### Inferences
- Holm (FWER) is the safer choice for confirmatory claims in a family of 4-8; BH is acceptable only if you explicitly accept FDR semantics, and the original BH proof assumes independence (note correlated comparisons from shared seeds/splits). Whichever is used, state the family definition before data are seen.
- With per-stage families of 4-8 and several stages, the study-wide FWER is not controlled. Options: (a) declare each stage's families as separate, state that cross-stage error is uncontrolled and label results "per-stage"; (b) apply an overall correction (Bonferroni across stages, alpha-spending); (c) designate one primary confirmatory claim per stage and treat the rest as exploratory. For an equivalence verdict, however, multiplicity is less damaging: claiming equivalence requires all conditions (intersection-union), whereas multiplicity inflates the chance of falsely claiming an advantage in any of k comparisons. So error inflation matters mainly for "advantage" claims, which is a conservative error direction for a "no advantage" paper.
- Testing the same hypothesis at several stages (e.g. a hypothesis revisited after stage-5 results shape stage 6) is the forking-paths situation: choices were data-contingent even if each is stated. Preregistration of later stages after seeing earlier results is only partially confirmatory; label it as such (cf. Nosek et al. on existing data).
- Preregistration alone does not prevent reporting issues; checklist and seed/split variance practices (Pineau, Bouthillier, Agarwal, Henderson) are the relevant standards for ML experiments. The sources recommend more seeds, multiple splits, and bootstrap CIs: confirm the number of seeds/splits is sufficient so that the CI width is smaller than the 0.06-wide equivalence window.

## 3. Negative results, registered reports, and baseline/tuning pitfalls

### Takeaway
There is established venue support for negative results in ML, and a consistent literature shows that apparent advantages often vanish when baselines get equal tuning. These are strong arguments for the paper's design, but they impose a duty: demonstrate equal hyperparameter budgets and check tuning-grid edges.

### Cited Findings
- Karl, Kemeter, Dax, Sierak 2024, "Position: Embracing negative results in machine learning", arXiv:2406.03980 (abstract only): predictive performance alone is a poor measure of publication value; argues for publishing negative results; names ICBINB-type workshops as first steps. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03980)
- "I Can't Believe It's Not Better" (ICBINB) workshop series at NeurIPS since 2020; 2021 call asked for ideas that look promising but don't work with explanations; 2022 asked for stated hypotheses empirically falsified (search-result summaries, mailing-list calls). — [2021 CFP](https://mailman.srv.cs.cmu.edu/pipermail/connectionists/2021-August/036347.html); [2022 CFP](https://mailman.srv.cs.cmu.edu/pipermail/connectionists/2022-August/038485.html)
- Lucic et al. 2018, "Are GANs created equal?", NeurIPS, arXiv:1711.10337 (ar5iv read): with enough tuning and restarts most GANs reach similar FID; no algorithm consistently beats the non-saturating GAN; hyperparameter search was 100 samples over wide ranges vs 50 over narrow ranges; compare distributions of scores at a fixed compute budget; best-of-run reporting is "meaningless"; budget matters, and at small budgets significant comparisons are infeasible. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.10337)
- Musgrave, Belongie, Lim 2020, "A metric learning reality check", ECCV, arXiv:2003.08505 (ar5iv read): reported gains often arise from unequal architectures, embedding sizes, augmentation, and test-set model selection; under a controlled protocol (cross-validated Bayesian hyperparameter search, 10 runs with CIs) most losses perform similarly. Recommend separate validation set, same settings across methods, properly implemented baselines. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08505)
- Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi, Jannach 2019, "Are we really making much progress?", RecSys, arXiv:1907.06902 (ar5iv read, partial): 7 of 18 neural top-n recommenders reproducible; 6 of those 7 did not consistently beat well-tuned simple baselines; baselines tuned with 35 Bayesian-search configurations; some originals did not tune baselines or chose epochs on the test set. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.06902)

### Inferences
- These papers support the claim that null results are informative, but reviewers will ask whether the proposed method (and the baselines) received equal tuning budget, and whether any tuned optimum lay at the edge of the grid (Lucic's wide vs narrow range finding shows range choice changes conclusions). Report the grid, the budget per method, and the location of selected values; a null with a baseline favored by the grid, or a proposed method starved of tuning, is not an equivalence finding.
- Equivalence results should be reported alongside power/precision, since Lucic et al. show small budgets cannot support significant comparisons.

### Gaps
- Registered reports for ML specifically and the Journal of Negative Results / ML Reproducibility Challenge were not verified with primary sources (search returned no registered-report proposal for ML).
- Forde & Paganini 2019 ICBINB origin paper not verified.

## 4. LLM-based review and sub-agents as reviewers

### Takeaway
Same-model sub-agents are not independent error processes. Evidence shows LLMs from the same family/provider share errors and favor their own outputs, and that models are sycophantic. Same-model review is a useful check for obvious mistakes but cannot count as independent validation; disclose it as such.

### Cited Findings
- Zheng et al. 2023, "Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", NeurIPS D&B, arXiv:2306.05685 (ar5iv read): GPT-4 agreed with humans 85% on MT-bench (humans 81% among themselves; ties excluded). Biases: position (GPT-4 consistent in 65.0% of swapped pairs; Claude-v1 23.8%), verbosity (Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 failed a repetitive-list test 91.3%), and self-enhancement (GPT-4 ~10% higher win rate for itself, Claude-v1 ~25%) -- but the authors state data are insufficient to conclude self-enhancement exists. Math/reasoning: GPT-4 default prompt misjudged 14 of 20; reference-guided prompting cut this to 3 of 20. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- Panickssery, Bowman, Feng 2024, "LLM evaluators recognize and favor their own generations", arXiv:2404.13076 (ar5iv read): GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama 2 prefer their own summaries; GPT-4 identifies its own output at 73.5%; self-recognition correlates linearly with self-preference after finetuning; swapping source labels dropped GPT-4's self-preference from 0.73 to 0.32. Limits: two summarization datasets, three models; causation not proven; quality not controlled. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2404.13076)
- Kim, Garg, Peng, Garg 2025, "Correlated errors in large language models", ICML 2025 (PMLR 267), arXiv:2506.07962 (abstract read): over 350 models; models agree ~60% of the time when both err on one leaderboard; shared architecture and shared provider increase correlation; larger and more accurate models are more correlated even across providers; affects LLM-as-judge (judges overrate weaker models, bias largest within the same provider). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962); [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/kim25e.html)
- Kohli 2026, "Nine Judges, Two Effective Votes: Correlated Errors Undermine LLM Evaluation Panels", arXiv:2605.29800 (search summary only; a preprint, treat as unreviewed): nine frontier LLMs from seven families yield roughly two independent votes; best single judge matches or beats the panel. A same-model panel would be worse. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29800)
- Sharma et al. 2023, "Towards understanding sycophancy in language models", arXiv:2310.13548 (ar5iv read, ~100k of 129k): five assistants tailor feedback to a user's stated views; accuracy dropped up to 27% when the user challenged a correct answer; Claude 1.3 admitted mistakes on ~98% of challenged questions; preference data reward matching user beliefs. More capable models were affected less but not immune. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.13548)
- Liang et al. 2024, "Can large language models provide useful feedback on research papers?", NEJM AI (arXiv:2310.01783; search summary only): GPT-4 comments overlapped with human reviewers at roughly the same rate as two humans overlapped (30.85% vs 28.58% for Nature journals; 39.23% vs 35.25% for ICLR); 57.4% of 308 users rated feedback helpful (one secondary summary reports different figures, so verify). Another study on medical manuscripts found limited correlation with human reviews. — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10617069)
- Disclosure: ICMJE and COPE (per secondary commentary, not primary documents) say AI cannot be an author; use of AI should be disclosed, with writing assistance in acknowledgments and AI use in data analysis in Methods. The exact ICMJE section/version could not be confirmed (sources cite 2023 and January 2026 updates). — [Editage summary](https://www.editage.com/insights/the-icmje-recommendations-on-ai-advice-for-authors-and-peer-reviewers); [CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/can-ai-be-listed-as-an-author)

### Inferences
- Reviews by sub-agents of the same model are likely to share blind spots and to be sympathetic to the author's framing (sycophancy), especially if prompted with the author's conclusions. Do not describe them as "independent" in the statistical sense; call them "same-model, context-isolated reviews". Mitigations: give reviewers raw data and the preregistration rather than the conclusions, ask for adversarial refutation, use reference-guided prompts (Zheng: 14/20 to 3/20 errors), use a different model family if possible, and verify every flagged claim by rerunning code.
- Claims that reviews "corrected" stages carry weight only where corrections were checked by execution or human inspection. Liang et al. support LLM review as a pre-submission aid, not a substitute for human or other independent review.
- Disclose model name/version, prompts, what each reviewer saw, and what was changed as a result, in Methods, and state that the human author takes responsibility.

### Gaps
- No study located specifically testing same-model sub-agents as independent reviewers of computational studies; the inference rests on cross-model correlation results, which show correlation increases with shared provider.
- Primary ICMJE/COPE/venue policy pages were not read; confirm the target venue's AI policy.
- Liang et al. numbers come from search summaries; confirm in the paper.

## Summary of recommendations that suggest the approach is inadequate or needs hedging
1. Cross-stage family-wise error: no correction across stages; each stage's 4-8 tests are corrected only locally. Disclose or add a global correction; the risk is mostly for "advantage" claims.
2. Reusing the same hypothesis across stages after earlier results is a forking-paths exposure (Gelman & Loken); preregistration of later stages after seeing earlier data is partially exploratory (Nosek).
3. SESOI 0.03 must be justified and shown to be above the test-set noise floor (Lakens; Bouthillier); otherwise results are "inconclusive", not "equivalent".
4. Same-model sub-agent reviews are not independent (Kim; Panickssery; Kohli; Sharma); label accordingly.
5. Equal hyperparameter budget and grid-edge checks (Lucic; Musgrave; Dacrema) must be demonstrated to support a null.
6. Use 90% CIs for TOST at alpha=.05 (100-2*alpha when corrected); report bounds in raw units.
